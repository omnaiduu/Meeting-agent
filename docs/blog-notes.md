# Engineering blog notes

Raw notes for writing the engineering blog post about this project. Each entry is something interesting we found, a problem we hit, or a decision worth explaining. Newest at the bottom. Not polished prose: draft from these.

Tags: **[finding]** something we learned · **[problem]** something that broke and how we fixed it · **[decision]** a choice and why · **[side note]** fun detail worth a sidebar

---

## Before writing code (research)

- **[finding] Meet blocks plain headless Chrome.** Every open-source Meet bot we looked at runs a *headed* Chrome on Xvfb (an in-memory fake screen). That's why the bot needs a "real" browser on a server, not a scraping engine.
- **[finding] "Lightweight browsers" can't do this.** Lightpanda and similar engines skip WebRTC and audio, which is the one thing a meeting bot needs. The browser *is* the heavy part, and you can't avoid it.
- **[finding] Meet does not give one audio track per person.** It sends a few shared audio channels that carry whoever is speaking now. So "who said what" can't come from tracks alone. This is why speaker identification is deferred.
- **[finding] Zoom changed the rules on March 2, 2026.** Apps joining meetings outside their own account must be attributed to a user (OBF/ZAK token or RTMS). A guest-browser bot like ours can't just join Zoom anymore. Meet first, Teams (guest web join still works) second.
- **[side note] Vendor prices.** Recall.ai, MeetingBaaS, Skribby, Attendee and Vexa charge roughly $0.30–0.65 per bot-hour. Building our own costs only compute plus speech-to-text.
- **[decision] Go + chromedp instead of Python/Node + Playwright.** One small binary, goroutine per meeting, and chromedp's `ExposeFunc` lets in-page JavaScript call Go directly. That's our audio pipe. go-rod hasn't released since July 2024, and playwright-go needs a Node driver.
- **[decision] Capture audio inside the page instead of at the OS level.** Most bots route audio through PulseAudio and record with ffmpeg. We hook `RTCPeerConnection` in the page, mix the remote tracks with Web Audio, downsample to 16 kHz in an AudioWorklet, and stream PCM to Go. That means no PulseAudio, no ffmpeg, and fewer moving parts.
- **[side note] Groq bills speech-to-text in 10-second minimums**, so we send ~20 s chunks rather than tiny ones. That's cheaper, and Whisper gets more context per chunk.

## Building

- **[problem] Our cloud dev box can't reach Google Meet.** `meet.google.com` is blocked by the sandbox network, and WebRTC needs UDP anyway. So the real join test has to happen on a laptop. Inside the sandbox we test the bot against a **fake Meet page** we serve locally: same buttons and aria-labels, plus a WebRTC loopback that plays a known audio clip. That makes the bot testable in CI without Google. (Good blog section: "How do you test a bot for a service you can't reach?")
- **[side note]** The sandbox's Chrome is "Chrome for Testing" 153, the same build Google publishes for automation. Handy, because it matches what we'd pin in Docker.
- **[finding] "Leave call" is not proof that you're in the call.** The obvious signal for "the host admitted us" is the Leave call button. But Meet also renders it in the lobby, and Vexa hit exactly this bug: their bot thought it was admitted while it was still waiting. Better signals are things that only exist in the call: participant tiles (`[data-participant-id]`), the "Present now" / "Share screen" button, or remote audio tracks actually arriving over WebRTC. Our bot treats "remote audio track arrived" or a participant tile as admission.
- **[finding] Typographic apostrophes break text matching.** Meet's live copy says "You’ll join the call…" and "can’t join" with a curly ’, not '. Text matchers need both forms. We normalize ’ → ' before matching.
- **[finding] "Return to home screen" appears in the lobby too.** Vexa once treated it as "denied" and gave up after 4 seconds while the host hadn't decided yet. Only "denied your request" / "weren't allowed to join" mean denial.
- **[finding] Detecting "I'm alone in the meeting" is harder than it sounds.** Counting `[data-participant-id]` tiles can drop to 0 during a screen share or a layout change. Vexa's bot left healthy meetings after 60 s because of this (issue #285, April 2026). Their principled fix: only declare "alone" when two independent signals agree. We do the same: alone = people count ≤ 1 **and** no remote audio for a while.
- **[decision] Mark-then-click instead of hard-coded selectors in Go.** A small in-page "probe" script scans the DOM with all our text/aria patterns, decides what phase we're in (pre-join, lobby, in call, denied, ended), and tags the element to act on with `data-meet-agent="join-button"`. Go then clicks that tag with a real CDP mouse event. The fuzzy matching stays in one JS function (easy to unit-test against fake pages), and the actions stay real input events.
- **[problem] chromedp v0.24 is a different library from the tutorials.** In 2026 chromedp moved to a generics API: actions are `Action[T]`, you call `chromedp.Run[T]`/`chromedp.Do`, `chromedp.Evaluate[State](js)` decodes straight into a Go struct, and raw protocol commands are `chromedp.Call(ctx, page.SetBypassCSP, params)`. `ActionFunc` and `.Do(ctx)` from older blog posts don't compile anymore. It reads nicer once you know it.
- **[problem] Our browser died one second after launching.** The first chromedp call on a context *starts* Chrome and ties the browser's whole lifetime to that call's context. We made that first call with a 30-second timeout context, so as soon as the function returned and `cancel()` ran, Chrome was killed: "context canceled". The fix: make the first call with the long-lived tab context, then use timeouts for later calls only.
- **[finding] Calls from JS into Go can arrive out of order.** chromedp runs every `ExposeFunc` call in its own goroutine. Audio batch #7 can reach Go before #6, so each batch carries a sequence number and Go puts them back in order (`audio.Reorderer`), skipping ahead if one is lost.
- **[finding] Content Security Policy fights audio capture, twice.** Meet sends a strict CSP with Trusted Types. Our fake Meet sends a similar one, and we ran an experiment with the CSP bypass turned off:
  1. The AudioWorklet (loaded from a `blob:` URL) was blocked, so the code fell back to the older ScriptProcessorNode. Fine so far.
  2. But the recording was **0 seconds**. The fallback built its resampler class with `new Function(...)`, and CSP blocks that too (no `unsafe-eval`). Both paths were dead.

  Fixes: define the resampler as a normal class and pass `Resampler.toString()` into the worklet, so there's no eval anywhere and the fallback works under a strict CSP. In production we also call `Page.setBypassCSP` for the bot's tab, so the worklet loads normally. Belt and braces.
- **[finding] An AudioWorklet with no input produces no output, and that breaks timestamps.** When every remote track ended, the worklet stopped emitting samples, so the recording "skipped" the quiet part (15 s recorded for a 25 s call). Transcript timestamps are computed from sample counts, so they would drift every time people leave. The fix: when there's no input, emit silence. Now the recording length matches the wall clock (26.4 s for 26 s in the call).
- **[side note] How good is audio captured through WebRTC?** We played a known espeak clip into the fake meeting and cross-correlated the bot's recording with the original: **0.78 normalized correlation at 89 ms latency**, after a round trip through Opus (a lossy codec). That's plenty for speech-to-text.
- **[side note] The fake Meet copies Meet's traps on purpose.** Its lobby shows a "Leave call" button and "Return to home screen", and a "Continue without microphone and camera" pop-up covers the join button. Each trap is a regression test for a real bug other bots shipped.
- **[side note] We expected to need a Chrome flag for WebRTC loopback** (`WebRtcHideLocalIpsWithMdns`: Chrome hides local IPs behind mDNS names). We tested without it, and it wasn't needed for an in-page loopback, so we removed it. One less difference between test and production.
- **[side note] First real Whisper result.** We sent the bot's recording (captured through WebRTC → Web Audio → AudioWorklet → Go) to Groq `whisper-large-v3-turbo`, and it came back word for word: *"Hello everyone. Let's ship the new release on Friday. Priya will write the launch notes."* Groq took well under a second for 26 s of audio. Nice screenshot moment for the post.
- **[decision] Skip silence before it reaches Whisper.** Whisper was trained on lots of subtitled video, and on silence or noise it famously "hears" things like *"Thank you."* or *"Thanks for watching!"*. Two defenses: (1) chunks where no 100 ms window rises above about −46 dBFS are never sent, which also saves money because Groq bills a 10 s minimum per request; (2) segments with `no_speech_prob > 0.6`, or that are exactly a known hallucination phrase, are dropped. We check 100 ms windows rather than the whole chunk's average, so a single short "yes" in 20 s of quiet isn't thrown away.
- **[decision] Strict JSON schema instead of "JSON mode".** Groq supports OpenAI-style Structured Outputs (`response_format: json_schema, strict: true`) for `openai/gpt-oss-120b`. The model is constrained to our exact shape (title, overview, key_points, decisions, action_items with nullable owner/due, open_questions), so there's no "please return valid JSON" prompt begging and no repair step. The test summary came back in about 1.3 s, and owner/due were filled only when the transcript said them (Maria / Thursday) and null otherwise.
- **[side note] gpt-oss "thinks" before answering.** One tiny summary used 448 reasoning tokens for 139 tokens of output. Worth knowing for cost math; it's still cheap on Groq.
- **[decision] Long meetings: map/reduce.** If the transcript is over about 80k characters, each part is summarized with the same schema, and a final call merges the partial notes. Tested with a fake server: 2 parts + 1 merge = 3 calls.
- **[problem] "invalid context" after joining.** The bot joined fine, then the "is the call over?" watcher failed every probe with `invalid context`. Cause: chromedp actions must run on a context *derived from the tab context*. Our Stop button's context came from the server's root context, so it had no browser attached. The fix is a small pattern we now use twice: derive from the tab context, and cancel it when the stop context is cancelled.
- **[decision] SSE without losing lines.** On connect the handler (1) subscribes to the live hub *first*, (2) sends the current status, (3) replays transcript lines after `Last-Event-ID` (EventSource sends it automatically on reconnect), then (4) streams live events, skipping any transcript id it already sent. Subscribing before reading the DB closes the gap where a line could slip through; de-duping by id prevents doubles. Slow clients are dropped from the hub instead of blocking a bot. They reconnect and replay.
- **[decision] One ordered transcription worker per meeting, not a pool.** The plan said "2 workers". In practice a 20 s chunk transcribes in under a second, and a single worker keeps lines in order for free. Simple won.
- **[side note] The whole pipeline in one test.** `TestEndToEnd` starts the HTTP API, launches headless Chrome, joins the fake Meet, plays an espeak clip over real WebRTC, transcribes it, summarizes it, and reads the SSE stream until `done`. Status order observed: `queued → launching → joining → in_lobby → in_call → leaving → transcribing → summarizing → done`. About 22 s. With `E2E_GROQ=1` it uses the real Groq APIs.
- **[problem → fix] Fixed-length chunks cut words in half.** The first full UI run transcribed "the beta build passed QA yesterday, except one **crash** on older Android phones" as "…except one **day.**" / "crash on older android phones." The 10-second boundary landed inside the word "crash", so Whisper heard half a word at the end of one chunk and the rest at the start of the next. The fix: cut at the **quietest 100 ms window in the last 30% of the chunk**, i.e. in a pause between words. After the fix the same audio came out as "…passed QA yesterday. / Except one crash on older Android phones.", and "public *made* on Friday" became "public *beta* on Friday" too. Offsets still add up because each chunk's offset is the sum of the previous chunk lengths. A cheap stand-in for real voice activity detection, ~20 lines.
- **[side note] A fake meeting with a cast.** The demo fake Meet plays a 9-line product stand-up in three espeak-ng voices (`en-us`, `en-gb+f3`, `en-us+m3`). It's enough to demo the whole product, screenshot it, and screen-record it without a real meeting or real people. The summary model correctly kept "owner" empty for the crash fix (nobody's name was said) and filled "Maria / Thursday afternoon" for the release notes.
- **[decision] shadcn/ui without the shadcn CLI.** The sandbox couldn't reach shadcn's registry, which is a nice reminder of what shadcn actually is: not a dependency, just copy-pasted component source on top of Radix primitives. We wrote the eight components we need (Button, Input, Card, Badge, Tabs, Tooltip, Confirm dialog, Skeleton) on the unified `radix-ui` package with the same `cva` + `cn` pattern.
- **[side note] 2026 frontend versions.** Vite 8, React 19.3, TanStack Router 1.170, Tailwind 4.3, and **TypeScript 7.0, the native Go port of the compiler**. `tsc -b` on the whole app is near-instant. A Go backend, and a TypeScript compiler written in Go: a fun aside.
- **[decision] SSE events go straight into the TanStack Query cache.** The `useMeetingEvents` hook writes `status`/`transcript`/`summary` events into the same cache entries the page reads with `useQuery`. Components don't know whether data came from a fetch or the stream. The hook closes the EventSource when the meeting reaches `done`/`failed`. Otherwise the browser would happily reconnect forever.
- **[decision] Fonts are bundled, not loaded from Google.** Inter and JetBrains Mono come from `@fontsource-variable` and are embedded in the Go binary with the rest of the SPA. One binary, no third-party requests from the dashboard.
- **[problem] Raw Chrome logs in the UI.** The first failed meeting showed users `[4867:4885:1120/122341.865660:ERROR:dbus/bus.cc:405] Failed to connect to the bus…`. Now every failure has a short human message plus a hint ("Ask the host to admit 'Meeting Agent' from the lobby next time."), and the raw details go to the server log only.
- **[finding] No PulseAudio needed, even in Docker.** Every Meet bot guide we read installs PulseAudio with a virtual sink and records with ffmpeg, and our plan listed "no sound card in the container → silent recordings" as a risk. We tested it: in our Ubuntu image (no PulseAudio, no ALSA device) the AudioWorklet capture worked and Whisper transcribed the call perfectly. Chrome still runs the Web Audio graph without an output device, because we never need to *hear* the audio, only process it. That's a whole moving part (and its configuration) gone from the image.
- **[decision] Chrome for Testing instead of `google-chrome-stable`.** The apt package always gives "latest", so builds aren't reproducible. Google's Chrome for Testing publishes exact versioned builds as zip files. The image pins `CHROME_VERSION=153.0.8010.12`, the same version our tests ran against. Bumping it is a deliberate one-line change.
- **[decision] `tini` as PID 1.** Chrome forks a dozen processes (renderer, GPU, network, audio…). Without an init process, killed ones become zombies in a long-running container. `tini` reaps them and forwards SIGTERM to the server, which triggers our graceful shutdown.
- **[side note] Graceful shutdown, measured.** `docker stop` in the middle of a call: the server tells every bot to leave, the bot clicks "Leave call", flushes the last audio, transcribes it, summarizes, and marks the meeting `done` (end reason "stopped") in **4.2 s**. Docker's default stop timeout is 10 s, which is enough here but not for a long meeting's summary, so compose sets `stop_grace_period: 60s`. Anything killed harder is marked `failed: interrupted` on the next start, never left "in call" forever.
- **[problem] Building Docker images in a locked-down sandbox.** Our dev sandbox only reaches the internet through an HTTPS proxy with its own CA, and Debian's mirrors weren't on the allowlist, but Ubuntu's were. Two choices made it work without polluting the real Dockerfile: (1) Ubuntu 24.04 as the runtime base, which is a perfectly good choice anyway; (2) a throwaway copy of the Dockerfile that adds the proxy CA and switches apt to HTTPS, built with `--network host` and BuildKit's `--build-context` to pass the CA file. The committed Dockerfile has none of that.
- **[side note] Image size: 1.36 GB**, almost all of it Chrome and fonts (Noto CJK for meetings with non-Latin names). The Go binary with the embedded dashboard is ~20 MB.
- **[side note] Recording the demo with Playwright.** The README GIF was made by a Playwright script that types the link, sends the bot, and watches the meeting with `recordVideo` on. ffmpeg then speeds up the 80-second meeting 4× and keeps the typing and the summary at normal speed. Result: 35 s, 0.7 MB MP4, 3.5 MB GIF. Re-recordable after any UI change with one command.

## After the build

- **[finding] The honest weak spot: the bot reads Meet's screen.** There is no official API for a bot to join a Meet call as a participant, so every bot of this kind (paid services included) recognizes the join button, lobby, "call ended" and so on from visible text and accessibility labels. We put every rule in one file and wrote down each rule, what it's for, and what happens when it breaks (`docs/LIMITATIONS.md`). Good blog angle: "what's hard-coded, and why that's the industry norm".
- **[decision] Design rule: each hard-coded rule should fail *soft*.** For example, if the "in call" labels change, the bot still knows it was admitted because it hears real audio. If the people count can't be read, the "alone" rule never fires and the bot leaves at "call ended" or the 2-hour limit instead of leaving early. Failing late beats failing wrong.
- **[idea] Self-healing selectors with an AI agent.** A daily canary bot joins a test meeting. When a rule stops matching, it saves the page's DOM and a screenshot and hands them to a coding agent (e.g. Claude Code) that updates the selector file, runs the fake-Meet tests, and opens a pull request for a human to review. Not built yet. Open problems: hosting a test meeting without a human to admit the bot, and run frequency and cost.
- **[side note] "Live" transcript, honestly.** Timestamps are exact (sample counting + Whisper segment times), but each line appears up to about 20 s after it's said, because Whisper works on chunks, not streams. 00:00 is when the bot was admitted, not when the meeting began.
